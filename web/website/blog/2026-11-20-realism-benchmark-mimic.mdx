Every healthcare data vendor asserts data quality. Almost nobody *measures* it — against a defined framework, on their own data, with numbers you can check. Assertions are marketing; a scorecard is a receipt.

This post is about a data-quality engine that grades its own homework. It implements the Patient Information Quality Improvement Framework — **PIQI**, pronounced "picky" — not as code but as FHIR resources, so every quality check is an inspectable, forkable artifact. Then, to prove it measures something real, we pointed it at MIMIC-IV-FHIR: a baseline scorecard on genuine ICU data, one honest intervention (auto-coding), and a re-score against ground truth we manufactured by removing codes we already had.

## PIQI in one section

PIQI comes from the PIQI Alliance (Charles Harp's framework guides, v1.1/v1.2), and its core move is to make "data quality" decomposable instead of vibes. Four **quality dimensions**, each with sub-classifications: **accuracy** (is the value right?), **conformity** (does it obey the rules of its type and structure?), **availability** (is it there at all — populated, complete?), and **plausibility** (is it believable — a 3,000 mmHg blood pressure is well-formed and populated and wrong).

The unit of measurement is the **SAM** — a Simple Assessment Module: a pass/fail check bound to a data attribute. `Attr_IsPopulated` on a lab value, `Concept_HasCode` on a coded concept, `Attr_IsNumeric` on something that must parse as a number. Each SAM declares its scoring effect, an optional weight, and an optional *criticality* flag.

A **rubric** is an ordered, weighted set of SAMs scoped to data classes (conditions, medications, lab results, demographics…). Run a rubric over a payload and you get a **scorecard**: the **PIQI Index** (0–100, passed items over scorable items), a weighted variant when the rubric weights SAMs, a **critical failure count** for SAMs flagged critical, and a **Data Class Frequency** breakdown so you can see *which* classes dragged the score. One thing the framework deliberately does not give you is interpretive bands — there is no universal "75 is good." A score means what its rubric makes it mean; comparison is rubric-relative. That's a feature: it forces you to say what you care about.

## The implementation: PIQI as FHIR resources

Here is the part we think is genuinely new. Most quality frameworks live in code — a Python package, a rules engine, a vendor product. Ours lives in **data**:

- **Every SAM is a ViewDefinition** (SQL-on-FHIR v2) that defines the extraction shape — *which* elements of which resources constitute the population under assessment — **composed with a SQLQuery Library** (the analytics-layer profile) that carries the pass/fail predicate.
- **Rubrics are SQLQuery Libraries** whose `relatedArtifact` dependencies point at the SAM queries they weigh and order.
- Execution runs through the standard fhir4ds SQL-on-FHIR analytics layer — **zero custom runtime**. If you can run a ViewDefinition, you can run a PIQI scorecard. Scoring is deterministic, and every result carries provenance: which SAM fired, on which elements, with what evidence — an audit trail, not a verdict from a black box.

We implemented 33 of the 37 guide-defined SAMs, which per the guides is a complete implementation with documented exclusions (the remaining four are builder-trivial-unbound or RESTful-surface checks). Because the whole thing is resources, anyone can inspect a rubric, re-weight it for their own priorities, and fork it — the framework's rubric-relativity made mechanical.

Two integrations matter in practice. Every verdict feeds a **curation queue**: near-misses and vocabulary-unmatched resources get routed for human review instead of silently bucketed. And the scorecard is *attributive* — it doesn't just say "38," it says which SAMs failed on which classes, which is what makes it an engineering instrument rather than a grade.

## The self-measuring engine, demonstrated on MIMIC

To show the engine measuring something real, we ran it against MIMIC-IV-FHIR — the Beth Israel ICU dataset converted to FHIR R4, 100 patients, roughly 900K resources of genuinely messy clinical content — through our deterministic, seeded realism harness (`fhir4ds.realism`). No synthetic corpus here: real UCUM oddities, real local code dictionaries, real missingness.

### The honest baseline

The scorecard on a 12-patient slice (86,224 resources, 98,202 scored items) read **38.5** on the broad default rubric — and **23.2** on the coding-centric rubric. Low numbers, but read the attribution before judging: the deductions are dominated by *genuinely absent* lab values and units (`Attr_IsPopulated` failing on 74,667 items), the real-world incompleteness synthetic corpora never model. The scorecard isn't wrong; the data is incomplete, and the scorecard correctly attributes the problem. That's the difference between a measurement and an insult.

A second self-measurement from the same corpus: deduplication over the full export found **46,469 duplicate clusters** — all real repeated clinical events, none injected — with **zero cross-patient clusters**, the hard safety invariant.

### One intervention, measured honestly: auto-coding

Then we changed one thing and re-ran the identical scorecard. The intervention: the medterm4ds auto-coder, which augments text-only `CodeableConcepts` with standard terminology codes (skipping concepts already coded in standard terminologies).

**The medication story is the cleanest.** MIMIC ships medications as name-codes — the coding's `code` literally is the drug name ("Ondansetron") under a private system, no display, no standard vocabulary. Measured on the coding columns: zero of 2,429 medication requests recognized, displayed, or complete before. After auto-coding, **360 carry RxNorm codings** — recognition, display, and completeness moving together, local drug names becoming addressable standard terminology. The auto-coder is conservative by contract: it refuses when it isn't confident, and every generated coding carries machine-readable provenance (engine, version, search mode, score, match grade, `userSelected=false`).

What auto-coding can and cannot recover falls into a clean three-tier taxonomy:

| tier | population (n) | recognized before → after |
| --- | --- | --- |
| already standard | procedures with SNOMED originals (224 of 494) | recognized as-shipped; auto-coding adds nothing |
| alias-recovered | conditions and procedures under MIMIC's wrapped ICD systems (669 + 121) | 0 → 669 conditions (100%); procedures 224 → 345 via environment aliases |
| translation-required | medications as local name-codes (2,429) | 0 → 0 without help; auto-coding moves 360 to RxNorm |

The conditions tier has its own lesson. MIMIC's ICD-9/10 codes sit under wrapper URIs, so they initially scored as unrecognized — fixed not by code but by an *environment alias*, a declarative table mapping wrapper URIs to their standard ICD counterparts, consulted after the standard-registry spellings. With aliases registered, every ICD condition is recognized before auto-coding touches anything; what auto-coding adds there is portability (SNOMED equivalents any consumer trusts) and clinical semantics, not a recognition delta.¹

**The text-gate finding.** PIQI *as scored* shows no movement in that production arm — and that is itself a result. The packaged Concept assessments are gated on `code.text` being populated, and MIMIC ships none. The scorecard told us exactly *where* the problem lives: value in the coding columns, silence in the scored column. (The scorecard also caught us: an earlier run under-counted recognized procedures because ICD-10-PCS was missing from the standard code-system registry. The engine grading its own homework includes grading ours.)

### Ground truth: the strip experiment

For real accuracy receipts we manufactured the legacy situation every EHR migration faces. We stripped codings from 1,643 coded resources (669 conditions, 494 procedures, 480 lab results — keeping display text as `code.text`), ran the scorecard, let the auto-coder reconstruct codes from text, and re-ran the identical scorecard. Because *we* removed the codes, we know exactly what the right answer was.

A counterfactual "ceiling" run — stripped corpus with original codings quietly re-attached — scored 41.7, isolating the 3,083 fail items that purely represent lost codings. The recall-oriented configuration wrote 3,366 codings across 1,216 of 1,643 stripped elements and recovered **2,188 of the 3,083 fail items — 71% of the coding gap, zero regressions**: nothing that passed before regressed, plausibility checks included. The strict default wrote only 28 codings — all `certain`-grade — recovering exactly 28 items with zero new failures. Conservative by design.

And the accuracy receipt: on the subset whose originals were standard SNOMED codes, the auto-coder recovered the **exact original (system, code) pair for 224 of 224 — 100% top-1 exact recovery**. On MIMIC-local dictionaries exact match is impossible by construction; instead it returns standard equivalents. Spot-checks: "Anemia in chronic kidney disease" (local ICD-9) → SNOMED 707323002 *Anemia in chronic kidney disease*; "Portal vein thrombosis" → SNOMED 17920008 *Portal vein thrombosis*. Original text preserved, provenance on every coding.

We also ablated the terminology engine's three search modes on the identical strip-and-gold slice: semantic maximizes coverage but its exact-code recovery collapses (2.4%); lexical anchors verbatim text but covers only half the corpus; **hybrid delivers semantic-grade coverage with the best exact recovery (13.6%)**, which is why it's the default.

## Honest limits

- **Four SAMs unimplemented**, each exclusion documented (builder-trivial-unbound or RESTful-surface checks).
- **Probable-grade suggestions need human confirmation.** The recall arm writes `probable`-and-better matches; that's routed to the curation queue deliberately. A terminology engine that never needs review is a terminology engine you shouldn't trust.
- **Recognized-system is environment-registry-relative.** Recognition depends on the code-system registry and alias table of the environment doing the scoring — which is the right semantics (a wrapper URI means what your environment says it means), but cross-environment comparisons must account for it.
- **We found gaps in the guides themselves and flagged them upstream** — a `HasType` reference with no defining guide, and ICD-10-PCS missing from the standard code-system closure. Framework adoption is a two-way street; finding and reporting the spec's own defects is part of the contribution.

The whole loop ships in fhir4ds: PIQI rubrics as resources, the realism harness (`fhir4ds.realism`) and the PIQI auto-coding benchmark (`fhir4ds.realism.piqi_autocoding`) — deterministic, repeatable, corpus-agnostic. Point them at your own exports. The interesting bugs are the ones your test data doesn't contain, and the scorecard will tell you exactly where they live.

¹ The alias table ships as data in the PIQI code-system registry; a different environment registers its own wrappers without code changes, and recognition through an alias is distinguishable from a standard spelling in the verdict's provenance.
