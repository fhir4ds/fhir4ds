Healthcare data arrives duplicated, abbreviated, and inconsistently spelled — usually all three at once. Before you can compute anything trustworthy, two questions have to be answered millions of times over:

<!-- truncate -->

1. **Are these two patient records the same human?**
2. **Are these two observations the same clinical event?**

fhir4ds answers both deterministically, with an audit trail, and without ever modifying your source data. This post shows how — with the actual rules, and before/after examples for the scenarios that show up in real exports.

## Part 1: The thirty rules of person matching

The matching engine is modeled on the CMS data-matching proposal: a cascade of ~30 deterministic rules, each one a specific combination of agreeing fields. The rules are tried **in order of statistical strength** — a rule whose field combination would collide by chance in one-in-a-quadrillion records gets to decide before a rule whose combination collides once in a billion. The first rule that fires wins, and its strength becomes the confidence of the match.

A sample of the cascade (strongest first):

| Rule | Fields that must agree | Chance collision |
|---|---|---|
| 22 | Any identifier in the same namespace | ~1 in 1,000,000,000,000,000 |
| 02 | First name (fuzzy) + last name (fuzzy) + DOB (±1 day) + **exact phone** | ~1 in 100 trillion |
| 04 | First name (fuzzy) + exact last name + exact DOB + **SSN last-4** | ~1 in 1,000,000,000,000 |
| 17 | Exact phone + **exact Medicare MBI** | ~1 in 1,000,000,000,000 |
| 11 | Exact first name + exact DOB + exact phone | ~1 in 500,000,000,000 |

Any rule whose combination is *rarer* than the approval threshold (a chance collision probability below 2×10⁻¹²) declares a **match**. Weaker agreement leaves the pair **ambiguous** rather than guessed. Nothing above the threshold is ever silently declared a non-match either — ambiguous means "a human should look", no_match means the cascade ran out of rules.

Three pieces of vocabulary make those "fuzzy" markers work:

- **Normalization happens first.** `MONTAVON ,` and `Montavon` and `Joé` are compared as canonical forms — case-folded, punctuation-stripped, diacritics-folded, suffixes (`Jr.`, `III`) pulled aside.
- **Nicknames are equivalence sets.** Joe ≡ Joey ≡ Joseph. The engine carries ~220 nickname sets, so "JOE" and "Joel" agreeing counts as first-name agreement.
- **Fuzzy means one typo.** Names match fuzzily at edit distance ≤ 1 — one dropped letter, one transposition. Birthdates match at ±1 day, which catches the overwhelming majority of fat-fingered DOBs.

### Scenario 1: The same patient, three spellings

**Before** — two records from two systems:

```json
{"id": "pat-a", "name": [{"family": "Montavon", "given": ["Joel"]}],
 "birthDate": "1975-03-12",
 "telecom": [{"system": "phone", "value": "(555) 867-5309"}]}

{"id": "pat-b", "name": [{"family": "MONTAVON ", "given": ["Joe"]}],
 "birthDate": "1975-03-11",
 "telecom": [{"system": "phone", "value": "555.867.5309"}]}
```

After normalization, last names are identical, given names are nickname-equivalent, birthdates are one day apart, and the phones — once placeholders are stripped — are **exactly** equal. Rule 02 (first~ + last~ + dob~ + phone=) fires at P ≈ 10⁻¹⁴, far below the 2×10⁻¹² threshold.

**After** — a match, recorded as a FHIR Linkage resource (the standard's "same entity" assertion), plus an audit row naming rule 02 and the evidence:

```json
{"resourceType": "Linkage", "active": true,
 "item": [
   {"type": "source",    "resource": {"reference": "Patient/pat-a"}},
   {"type": "alternate", "resource": {"reference": "Patient/pat-b"}}]}
```

The two Patient records are untouched. Reverse the Linkage and you're exactly where you started.

### Scenario 2: The sibling trap

**Before** — two brothers, same household, one letter apart:

```json
{"id": "bro-1", "name": [{"family": "Nguyen", "given": ["Danh"]}],
 "birthDate": "1998-04-02",
 "telecom": [{"system": "phone", "value": "555-222-1234"}]}

{"id": "bro-2", "name": [{"family": "Nguyen", "given": ["Dan"]}],
 "birthDate": "1996-04-02",
 "telecom": [{"system": "phone", "value": "555-222-1234"}]}
```

Same last name, same phone, given names at edit distance 1 — a pure rule-cascade could happily merge two different people on the phone-and-name rules. Real registries are full of exactly this shape: siblings, parent/child with the same name, roommates.

The cascade holds here on its own: the strongest name-and-phone rule (rule 30) requires the birthdates to match *exactly*, and no weaker rule can carry a two-year DOB gap plus a fuzzy-only first name below the threshold. And for the narrower trap — siblings or relatives who *do* share a birthdate, or a DOB typo that lands on the same day — rule 30 carries **disagreement guards**, a fhir4ds addition to the upstream proposal: before accepting the match, the engine verifies the SSN last-4s don't contradict each other and the first names genuinely agree. A candidate that fails a guard is dropped from that rule's survivors rather than negated outright.

**After** — `ambiguous`, deliberately. The engine surfaces contradiction candidates like this for human review instead of guessing, because merging two brothers is a much more expensive error than flagging a pair for a look.

## Part 2: Resource deduplication — same event, twice

Patient identity is only half the problem. The same *clinical event* gets recorded twice constantly: an interface retries a message, two systems both report the same lab, a timezone gets mangled in transit.

The dedup pipeline asks a narrower question — "could these be the same measurement/procedure/condition for the same patient?" — in two stages.

**Stage 1: Could they be the same event?** Records are bucketed by patient, type, date, and *canonical value*. Codes normalize across terminologies (LOINC ↔ SNOMED for the same concept), and quantities normalize across units — the UCUM math runs inside the engine.

**Stage 2: Are they close enough in time?** Every timestamp becomes a real instant — `08:30-06:00` and `14:30Z` are the same moment, a naive `08:30` gets a documented default zone rather than a guess. Then type-specific windows apply: vital signs merge within 30 minutes, serial troponins within 5, imprecise dates (month-only, year-only) subsume into any event their period contains.

### Scenario 3: Two words for "body weight," two units for 82 kg

**Before:**

```json
{"id": "obs-1", "code": {"coding": [
    {"system": "http://loinc.org", "code": "29463-7"}]},
 "effectiveDateTime": "2026-03-04T08:30:00-06:00",
 "valueQuantity": {"value": 82, "unit": "kg"}}

{"id": "obs-2", "code": {"coding": [
    {"system": "http://snomed.info/sct", "code": "27113001"}]},
 "effectiveDateTime": "2026-03-04T08:52:00-06:00",
 "valueQuantity": {"value": 82000, "unit": "g"}}
```

Different code systems, different units, 22 minutes apart. A human reads "same weight." Stage 1 sees both code sets canonicalize to *body weight* and both values convert to the same quantity. Stage 2 sees 22 minutes — inside the vital-signs window.

**After** — one cluster, one **survivor**, and an N-way Linkage:

```json
{"resourceType": "Linkage", "active": true,
 "item": [
   {"type": "source",    "resource": {"reference": "Observation/dedup-3f9a…"}},
   {"type": "alternate", "resource": {"reference": "Observation/obs-1"}},
   {"type": "alternate", "resource": {"reference": "Observation/obs-2"}}]}
```

The survivor is chosen by a transparent ranking — most precise timestamp first, then earliest, then richest clinical detail, with deterministic tie-breaks. It's a new merged resource (id `dedup-3f9a…`) carrying the union of both records' identifiers and codings, plus an extension recording the decision: cluster size, member references, and the rule that picked the survivor. The originals are, again, untouched.

### Scenario 4: The honest duplicate that *isn't*

**Before** — same patient, same test, 22 minutes apart… but serial troponins this time, where the hospital draws them 20 minutes apart on purpose:

```json
{"id": "trop-1", "code": {"coding": [
    {"system": "http://loinc.org", "code": "10839-9"}]},
 "effectiveDateTime": "2026-03-04T09:00:00-06:00",
 "valueQuantity": {"value": 14, "unit": "ng/mL"}}

{"id": "trop-2", "code": {"coding": [
    {"system": "http://loinc.org", "code": "10839-9"}]},
 "effectiveDateTime": "2026-03-04T09:18:00-06:00",
 "valueQuantity": {"value": 38, "unit": "ng/mL"}}
```

**After** — two observations, no merge. Rising troponins 18 minutes apart are *medicine*, not duplication: troponin clusters use a 5-minute window precisely so a serial draw is never collapsed into one row. (Flip side: the identical values in Scenario 3 at 6 minutes apart on a non-serial vital *still* merge — windows are per-code-family.) Every window boundary is inclusive and tested — 30:00 merges, 30:01 doesn't — and any *false* merge anywhere in the golden test corpus is treated as a release blocker, not a tuning knob.

## The point

None of this is machine learning guessing at similarity. It's ~30 published rules with computed collision probabilities, unit-aware quantity math, timezone-correct instant comparison, and windows per clinical family — all deterministic, all auditable, all reversible. Every decision writes its evidence before it writes its verdict.

The result is that when your analytical query asks "average body weight per patient in March," the answer counts Joel once and his March 4 weight once — and if anyone ever asks *why*, the audit table answers in one row.
