A clinical quality measure is a good stress test for a query engine. You take an
HL7 CQL library — hundreds of definitions, value sets, temporal intervals,
polymorphic FHIR fields — compile it to one population query, and run it over an
entire patient cohort at once. Nothing is cached between measures, and nothing
is pre-flattened: the engine reads raw FHIR JSON.

We wanted a number for "how does this behave as the cohort grows", and we wanted
to know *where the time goes* rather than just how much of it there was. So we
built a ladder: the same three measures, at 100, 1,000 and 5,000 patients, with a
query profile captured at every rung.

## Method, and one thing we deliberately did not count

The headline metric is **execution time only**. CQL parsing, library translation,
population-SQL generation and result conversion are measured and reported — but
separately, as compile diagnostics. They never enter a speedup or a
milliseconds-per-patient figure. Those phases are one-time per library version;
a production deployment pays them at deploy, not at query.

- **Engine:** FHIR4DS compiles each measure's CQL into a single population SQL
  query over an in-memory DuckDB table of FHIR resources.
- **Timing:** median of three executions of the *same pre-compiled* SQL. DuckDB
  re-plans on every execution, so planning is inside the number; compilation is
  not.
- **Cohorts:** nested deterministic prefixes of one Synthea generation (100 /
  1,000 / 5,000 patients), measurement period 2026 calendar year, one fresh
  process per rung, corpus loaded from disk once per rung.
- **Baseline:** the clinical-reasoning engine (Java, HAPI-based) evaluated
  per patient, **linearly extrapolated** from its per-patient cost. We did not
  run it at these sizes. That matters, and it is the first caveat below.

## The ladder

| Patients | Resources | Load | Peak RSS |
|---|---|---|---|
| 100 | 115,509 | 12 s | 2.2 GB |
| 1,000 | 1,223,154 | 126 s | 13.6 GB |
| 5,000 | 6,275,114 | 836 s | 43.9 GB |

Three measures, chosen to span the complexity range: **CMS68** (documentation of
current medications, a handful of retrieves), **CMS130** (colorectal cancer
screening, many retrieves), and **CMS2** (depression screening, the largest CQL
library of the three).

| Measure | 100 patients | 1,000 patients | 5,000 patients |
|---|---|---|---|
| CMS68 | 90 ms | 601 ms | 5,200 ms |
| CMS130 | 4,618 ms | 49,826 ms | 255,467 ms |
| CMS2 | 1,972 ms | 4,140 ms | 14,595 ms |

Per patient, that is ~1 ms for CMS68, ~51 ms for CMS130 and ~3 ms for CMS2 at the
largest rung. Against the extrapolated Java baseline the speedups are 691x,
13x and 41x respectively.

**Note the shape, not just the size.** CMS130 gets *more expensive per patient*
as the cohort grows, while CMS2 gets *cheaper* (19.7 ms/patient at 100 patients,
2.9 ms at 5,000). A columnar engine amortizes fixed costs over more rows — but
only if the work is proportional to rows that survive. When it is proportional to
rows *scanned*, more data just means more scanning.

## Two measures, 17x apart, and why

Static analysis of the generated SQL ranks the three measures in the right order —
but badly understates the gap. We scored the SQL structurally (scan sites,
`SELECT DISTINCT`, `json_extract`, date casts, joins, derived tables) and got
31 / 238 / 88 for CMS68 / CMS130 / CMS2. The measured cost ranking matches. The
*magnitude* does not: CMS130's score is 2.7x CMS2's, but its measured cost is
17.5x.

The missing term is **how many rows each predicate runs over**. Multiply scan
sites by the cohort's row count for each resource type and you get "rows
evaluated": 1.6M / 29.5M / 8.2M. That is 3.6x between CMS130 and CMS2 — closer,
still not 17.5x. What remains is per-row predicate cost, and that only shows up
in a profile.

So we took one. `EXPLAIN ANALYZE` at every rung:

| Measure | Scans | Rows scanned | Amplification | Scan share of time |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| CMS68 | 7 | 43,913,510 | 7.0x | 63.8% |
| CMS130 | 42 | 263,491,300 | 42.0x | 99.5% |
| CMS2 | 18 | 112,929,524 | 18.0x | 86.1% |

Amplification here means: the 5,000-patient corpus holds 6,275,114 rows, and
CMS130's query reads 263,491,300 of them. **42 times.** The engine re-reads the
whole corpus once per retrieve. CMS130 declares eleven Encounter retrieves, ten
Condition, eight Observation, five Procedure — and each one is emitted as its own
full table scan.

And it is *rung-invariant*: 41.5x at 100 patients, 42.0x at 5,000, with an
identical number of scans executed. That is the cleanest statement of the model:
**cost is O(scan sites x corpus), with a fixed multiplier.** Adding patients does
not change the multiplier; it just multiplies.

Critically, the work is in the *scans*, not in deduplication. Every retrieve CTE
deduplicates on the full JSON payload, and that dedup accounts for 0.2% of
operator time in CMS130, 4.1% in CMS2 and 13.0% in CMS68. Anyone optimizing
`SELECT DISTINCT` first — we did consider it — would be optimizing 0.2% of the
problem in the expensive measure.

## Two hypotheses the profile killed

Worth recording, because both were intuitive and both were wrong.

**"The value-set and interval calls are the expensive part."** CMS2 contains far
more value-set membership, coding-match and interval calls than CMS130 (8,013
versus 749 secondary counters; 1,178 versus 207 interval calls). It is also three
times cheaper per resource. The calls are not the cost — *where they run* is. In
CMS2 they sit behind selective filters over small candidate sets. In CMS130 the
same kind of predicate is pushed into a scan filter over 2.6 million Observations
and runs there, per row, several times, emitting zero rows.

**"Deduplicating the full JSON payload is a major win."** See above: 0.2-13% of
operator time. Real, but nowhere near the top of the list.

## Memory is a cliff, not a slope

At 5,000 patients the measure corpus needs more than 33 GB. At a 32 GB DuckDB
`memory_limit` it spills; at 48 GB it does not:

| Limit | CMS68 | CMS130 | CMS2 | Peak RSS |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 32 GB | 9,311 ms | 306,481 ms | 20,118 ms | 33.8 GB |
| 48 GB | 5,200 ms | 255,467 ms | 14,595 ms | 43.9 GB |

CMS68 improves 44%, CMS130 17%, CMS2 28% just from not spilling. One operational
detail that cost us time: an in-memory DuckDB database still needs
`temp_directory` set, or exceeding the memory limit fails instead of spilling.

## What we would do about it

Ranked by expected payoff, from the profile — and **none of it is implemented
yet**. This post is a measurement, not a changelog.

1. **A per-retrieve shared CTE cache.** Several CQL definitions retrieve the same
   resource type with the same filter, and each becomes a separate full scan.
   Deduplicating those retrieve scans attacks the 42x directly.
2. **Move heavy predicate work out of full-type scan filters.** CMS130's two most
   expensive operators are Observation scans evaluating nested `CASE` /
   `fhirpath_text` / interval / precision-aware comparison expressions per row,
   returning nothing. The translator already precomputes `period_start` /
   `period_end` columns; extending that to derived temporal bounds would let the
   cheap selectivity check run first.
3. **Fix the `_patients` CTE**, which still reads the whole 6.2-million-row table
   to produce 5,000 patient ids, and accounts for ~27% of operator time in
   CMS68's profile.
4. **Stop re-serializing JSON to text at every UDF call site.** The plan shows
   `fhirpath_text(CAST(resource AS VARCHAR), ...)` inside pushed-down filters.
   CMS130's SQL has 845 `fhirpath_text` call sites and 200 date-normalization
   sites. A VARCHAR shadow column removes the conversion.
5. **Dedupe on identity rather than payload** — worth doing once (1) lands,
   because it shrinks each CTE's payload, but explicitly *not* a priority on its
   own.

## Read these numbers with three caveats

We would rather publish the caveats than have someone discover them.

1. **The Java baseline is extrapolated, not measured.** It is the reference
   engine's per-patient cost multiplied by cohort size. Per-patient evaluation
   does scale roughly linearly, but this is an extrapolation and is labelled as
   one in the source data.
2. **Population counts in this benchmark are raw per-definition truth values**,
   with no CQM attribution masking applied. On synthetic data the numerator can
   exceed the denominator. They are sanity signals that the query ran, not
   reported measure rates — accuracy is validated separately against the official
   HL7 test bundles (47/47 QI-Core 2025 and 73/73 US-Core 2025).
3. **Operator shares under parallelism are not wall-clock seconds.** Per-operator
   timings are summed across worker threads; the shares show relative cost
   distribution. Wall time is the measured execution figure.

The full report — every rung, every operator bucket, the structural scoring
method and the compile diagnostics — is in
[`benchmarks/scaling/SCALING.md`](https://github.com/fhir4ds/fhir4ds/blob/dev/benchmarks/scaling/SCALING.md)
in the repository, regenerated by `benchmarks/scaling/make_report.py` from the
raw run data.

The short version for anyone building on a SQL-native clinical engine: the
standards are not what makes a measure expensive. Access patterns are. A measure
that reads its corpus 42 times will cost 42 corpora' worth of time no matter how
elegant the CQL is — and you will only find that out by profiling the operators,
not by reading the SQL.
