A clinical quality measure is a good stress test for a query engine. You take an
HL7 CQL library — hundreds of definitions, value sets, temporal intervals,
polymorphic FHIR fields — compile it to one population query, and run it over an
entire patient cohort at once. Nothing is cached between measures, and nothing is
pre-flattened: the engine reads raw FHIR JSON.

We wanted a number for "how does this behave as the cohort grows", and more
importantly *where the time goes*. So we built a ladder — the same measures at
100, 1,000 and 5,000 patients — with a query profile captured at every rung.

The profile pointed at something specific. We fixed it. This post is the
diagnosis, the fix, and the re-measurement.

## Method, and one thing we deliberately did not count

The headline metric is **execution time only**. CQL parsing, library translation,
population-SQL generation and result conversion are measured and reported — but
separately, as compile diagnostics. They never enter a speedup or a
milliseconds-per-patient figure. Those phases are one-time per library version;
a production deployment pays them at deploy, not at query.

- **Engine:** FHIR4DS compiles each measure's CQL into a single population SQL
  query over an in-memory DuckDB table of FHIR resources.
- **Timing:** median of repeated executions of the *same pre-compiled* SQL.
  DuckDB re-plans on every execution, so planning is inside the number;
  compilation is not.
- **Cohorts:** nested deterministic prefixes of one Synthea generation (100 /
  1,000 / 5,000 patients), measurement period 2026 calendar year, one fresh
  process per rung, corpus loaded from disk once per rung.
- **Baseline:** the clinical-reasoning engine (Java, HAPI-based) evaluated per
  patient, **linearly extrapolated** from its per-patient cost. We did not run it
  at these sizes. That matters, and it is the first caveat below.

## The ladder

| Patients | Resources | Load | Peak RSS |
|---|---|---|---|
| 100 | 115,509 | 12 s | 2.2 GB |
| 1,000 | 1,223,154 | 126 s | 13.6 GB |
| 5,000 | 6,275,114 | 836 s | 43.9 GB |

Three measures spanning the complexity range: **CMS68** (documentation of
current medications, a handful of retrieves), **CMS130** (colorectal cancer
screening, many retrieves), **CMS2** (depression screening, the largest CQL
library of the three).

| Measure | 100 patients | 1,000 patients | 5,000 patients |
|---|---|---|---|
| CMS68 | 90 ms | 601 ms | 5,200 ms |
| CMS130 | 4,618 ms | 49,826 ms | 255,467 ms |
| CMS2 | 1,972 ms | 4,140 ms | 14,595 ms |

Per patient at the largest rung: ~1 ms (CMS68), ~51 ms (CMS130), ~3 ms (CMS2).
Against the extrapolated Java baseline that is 691x, 13x and 41x.

**Note the shape, not just the size.** CMS130 got *more* expensive per patient as
the cohort grew (46 → 51 ms), while CMS2 got *cheaper* (19.7 → 2.9 ms). A
columnar engine amortizes fixed costs over more rows — but only when the work is
proportional to rows that survive. When it is proportional to rows *scanned*,
more data just means more scanning. That divergence was the first clue.

## What the profile found: the corpus read 42 times

Static analysis of the SQL ranks the measures correctly but badly understates the
gap. A structural score (scan sites, `SELECT DISTINCT`, `json_extract`, date
casts, joins, derived tables) gave 31 / 238 / 88 for CMS68 / CMS130 / CMS2. Order
correct. Magnitude wrong: CMS130 scored 2.7x CMS2 but measured 17.5x its cost.

The missing term is *how many rows each predicate runs over*. Multiply scan sites
by the cohort's row count per resource type and you get "rows evaluated" —
1.6M / 29.5M / 8.2M, a 3.6x ratio. Closer; still not 17.5x. The remainder is
per-row predicate cost, which only a profile shows.

So we profiled with `EXPLAIN ANALYZE`. At the 5,000-patient rung:

| Measure | Scans | Rows scanned | Amplification | Scan share of time |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| CMS68 | 7 | 43,913,510 | 7.0x | 63.8% |
| CMS130 | 42 | 263,491,300 | 42.0x | 99.5% |
| CMS2 | 18 | 112,929,524 | 18.0x | 86.1% |

The corpus held 6,275,114 rows and CMS130's query read 263 million of them. And
the ratio was **rung-invariant** — 41.5x at 100 patients, 42.0x at 5,000, with an
identical scan count. That is the cleanest statement of the model: cost was
`O(scan sites x corpus)` with a fixed multiplier. Adding patients did not change
the multiplier, it just multiplied.

Critically, the work was in the *scans*, not in deduplication. Every retrieve CTE
deduplicates on the full JSON payload, and that dedup accounted for 0.2% of
operator time in CMS130, 4.1% in CMS2 and 13.0% in CMS68.

## Two hypotheses the profile killed

Worth recording, because both were intuitive and both were wrong.

**"The value-set and interval calls are the expensive part."** CMS2 contained far
more value-set membership, coding-match and interval calls than CMS130 (8,013
versus 749 secondary counters; 1,178 versus 207 interval calls). It was also
three times cheaper per resource. The calls are not the cost — *where they run*
is. In CMS2 they sat behind selective filters over small candidate sets. In
CMS130 the same kind of predicate was pushed into a scan filter over 2.6 million
Observations and ran there, per row, several times, returning nothing.

**"Deduplicating the full JSON payload is a major win."** See above: 0.2-13% of
operator time. Real, but nowhere near the top of the list.

## Memory is a cliff, not a slope

At 5,000 patients the measure corpus needed more than 33 GB. At a 32 GB DuckDB
`memory_limit` it spilled; at 48 GB it did not:

| Limit | CMS68 | CMS130 | CMS2 | Peak RSS |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 32 GB | 9,311 ms | 306,481 ms | 20,118 ms | 33.8 GB |
| 48 GB | 5,200 ms | 255,467 ms | 14,595 ms | 43.9 GB |

CMS68 improved 44%, CMS130 17%, CMS2 28% just from not spilling. One operational
detail that cost us time: an in-memory DuckDB database still needs
`temp_directory` set, or exceeding the memory limit fails instead of spilling.

## Then we fixed the scans — and re-measured

The profile named the top item precisely: the same retrieve, over the same
resource type, re-executed as its own scan. So retrieves of one resource type now
share a **materialized per-resource-type base scan** (`_rt_Observation`,
`_rt_Encounter`, …), and every value-set CTE filters that materialization
instead of re-reading the table. In CMS130's current SQL there are **10
`FROM resources` sites** feeding **34 value-set retrieve CTEs** — where the
profile had seen 42 independent scans.

Re-running the identical rung-100 cohort on the current engine:

| Measure | Execution | Rows scanned | Amplification |
|---|---|---|---|
| CMS68 | 90 → **50 ms** (−44%) | 796,275 → **340,383** (−57%) | 6.9x → **2.9x** |
| CMS130 | 4,618 → **2,237 ms** (−52%) | 4,787,890 → **1,027,293** (−79%) | 41.5x → **8.9x** |
| CMS2 | 1,972 → **2,015 ms** (±0%) | 2,056,634 → **1,029,341** (−50%) | 17.8x → **8.9x** |

Two things worth reading off that table. First, CMS130 — the measure that was
41.5x — halved its wall time and cut rows scanned by 79%, which is what the
diagnosis predicted would happen. Second, **CMS2 barely moved in time despite
halving its rows scanned**. Removing scans does not automatically buy speed: once
the scan count drops, whatever per-row predicate work remains becomes the
dominant term. That is the next problem, and it is the one the profile can no
longer tell us about for free.

Amplification is now flat at ~8.9x across all three measures — the signature of a
fixed set of base scans rather than a per-retrieve multiplier.

## What is actually left

The original report ended with a ranked list of candidates. Reading it against
the current engine, the list has partly been overtaken:

1. **Per-retrieve shared scan — DONE**, as per-resource-type materialized base
   scans rather than a per-(type, filter) cache. Corpus re-reads are down 4.7x
   on CMS130.
2. **Stop re-serializing JSON to text at UDF call sites — DONE.** Current SQL
   calls `fhirpath_text(r.resource, ...)` and `in_valueset(r.resource, ...)`
   directly on the JSON column; there are zero `fhirpath_text(CAST(... AS
   VARCHAR))` sites left.
3. **Move heavy predicates out of full-type scan filters — PARTIAL.** Derived
   temporal columns *are* precomputed in the retrieve CTEs (`effective_start`,
   `effective_end`, `period_start`, `period_end`, `birth_date`). Whether the
   expensive interval and precision-aware comparisons still run inside the scan
   filter needs a fresh profile now that the scan structure changed.
4. **The `_patients` CTE still reads the whole table to produce a few thousand
   patient ids — OPEN.** It is `FROM resources AS p WHERE p.resourceType =
   'Patient'`, and it was ~27% of operator time in CMS68's profile.
5. **Dedupe on identity rather than payload — OPEN, and still not a priority.**
   Retrieve CTEs still `SELECT DISTINCT patient_id, resource, <derived columns>`.

Two follow-ups that are now more valuable than items 4-5: refresh the whole
ladder on the current engine (the numbers in this post's first half are from the
engine as it stood when the diagnosis was made), and do it on both supported
DuckDB lines, since materialization changes the memory profile and the 5,000
rung is where spilling starts.

## Read these numbers with four caveats

We would rather publish the caveats than have someone discover them.

1. **The ladder numbers are a snapshot of one engine revision.** The 100 /
   1,000 / 5,000 table and the 42x profile above were measured before materialized
   retrieve scans landed. The before/after table is a direct comparison at one
   rung; the larger rungs have not been re-run on the current engine.
2. **The Java baseline is extrapolated, not measured.** It is the reference
   engine's per-patient cost multiplied by cohort size. Per-patient evaluation
   does scale roughly linearly, but this is an extrapolation and is labelled as
   one in the source data.
3. **Population counts in this benchmark are raw per-definition truth values**,
   with no CQM attribution masking applied. On synthetic data the numerator can
   exceed the denominator. They are sanity signals that the query ran, not
   reported measure rates — accuracy is validated separately against the official
   HL7 test bundles (47/47 QI-Core 2025 and 73/73 US-Core 2025).
4. **Operator shares under parallelism are not wall-clock seconds.** Per-operator
   timings are summed across worker threads; the shares show relative cost
   distribution. Wall time is the measured execution figure.

The full report — every rung, every operator bucket, the structural scoring
method and the compile diagnostics — is in
[`benchmarks/scaling/SCALING.md`](https://github.com/fhir4ds/fhir4ds/blob/dev/benchmarks/scaling/SCALING.md)
in the repository, regenerated by `benchmarks/scaling/make_report.py` from the
raw run data.

The short version for anyone building on a SQL-native clinical engine: the
standards are not what makes a measure expensive. Access patterns are. A measure
that reads its corpus 42 times costs 42 corpora' worth of time no matter how
elegant the CQL is — and you will only find that out by profiling the operators,
not by reading the SQL. Then, once you have fixed it, profile again: the first fix
moves the bottleneck, it does not remove it.
