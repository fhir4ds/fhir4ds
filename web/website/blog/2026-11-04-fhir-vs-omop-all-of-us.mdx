A 2026 study from the All of Us Research Program compared
the same 29,512 participants' data as OMOP tables and as FHIR R4 payloads —
Patterson et al., *JAMIA* 33(9):1593–1604
([doi:10.1093/jamia/ocag095](https://doi.org/10.1093/jamia/ocag095)). The
headline numbers are stark:

| Median events per person | OMOP | FHIR |
|---|---|---|
| Measurement | 846 | 832 |
| Condition | 258 | 11 |
| Procedure | 72 | 4 |
| Participants with visits/encounters | 97% | 1.5% |

The conclusion many people draw — *OMOP is richer, FHIR is not ready for
research* — deserves a harder look, because the study itself tells you
exactly what it measured. It is careful, honest work — and with AMIA
ahead, it is likely to shape how people talk about FHIR analytics. Here is
what it did and did not
show, and the concrete answer to the barrier it identified.

## What the study actually compared

The two data streams reached the researchers through very different
pipelines:

- **OMOP** arrived through institution-level ETL, built per site by
  engineers who map concepts from *anywhere* in the EHR into the standard
  vocabulary, with an iterative submission-and-feedback loop until the data
  passed quality review.
- **FHIR** arrived as participants connected to FHIR providers through
  digital participant portals, which *"extract data via API from clinical
  datasets in the FHIR format"*; a data aggregator then *"moderately
  standardizes the data and submits it to AoURP"* — with, per the authors,
  *"no feedback cycle,"* and FHIR limited to *"standard mappings used in its
  default bulk export functionality,"* which are determined by the EHR
  vendor.

The authors say this themselves: *"clinical data documented in an unexpected
area of the EHR are often excluded from FHIR exports"* because vendor
defaults decide what leaves the building. But the underlying challenge is
not FHIR-specific. Clinical data that is never properly mapped and encoded —
documented in free text instead of coded into structured entries — is hard
to use in *either* standard; OMOP confronts exactly the same problem and
solves it by throwing institution-funded ETL engineers at the source. That
is a curation investment, not a model capability. FHIR itself is very rich
in the information it can hold and handle — when data is properly encoded
into its resources, nothing in the Condition or Procedure domains is beyond
it. The Condition 258-vs-11 gap is therefore a statement about acquisition
pipelines and encoding discipline, not about the expressive power of the
two data models.

The study also quantified a real and durable tradeoff: FHIR's blocking,
ingestion-time validation produced structurally cleaner data (unique ids,
resolvable references), while OMOP's non-blocking post-hoc checks traded
conformance for yield. Both findings are worth keeping.

## Where we agree

**OMOP is the right tool for observational research today.** It is
purpose-built for it: flat, regular tables, standardized concept ids, and a
decade of methods and tooling that assume its shape. If your institution
has already paid for an OMOP ETL, use it. We are not arguing against OMOP —
FHIR4DS (the open-source engine behind this blog) ships an OMOP CDM
mapping library, and the
[FHIR → OMOP walkthrough](/docs/examples/fhir-to-omop) exists precisely
because OMOP is often the destination downstream teams need.

**The gaps the study found are real — but they live in acquisition and
encoding, not in FHIR.** Data that is never properly mapped and coded —
documented in text rather than structured entries — yields poor research
data in *either* standard; OMOP overcomes it only through funded ETL
curation, and default FHIR exports simply haven't had that curation. The
encounter gap is the clearest case: the study's window (January 2020 –
March 2024) sat entirely inside the **USCDI v1** regulatory baseline,
which had no Encounter data class at all — encounters only entered USCDI
in **v2 (2021)**, and only as a voluntary update; the mandatory
certification baseline (USCDI v3, per the HTI-1 rule) took effect
**January 1, 2026**. The finding that just 1.5% of participants had FHIR
Encounters (versus 97% with OMOP visits) measures a v1-era export floor,
not a FHIR limitation — and the mandate the authors said would fix it
had already taken effect by the time the paper published.

## Where the framing needs a correction

The headline many people take away — "OMOP has 20× more clinical data than
FHIR" — conflates the data model with the pipeline that filled it. The fair
statement is:

> Institutionally-funded ETL currently yields more research-ready data than
> zero-configuration vendor exports, regardless of the destination model.

Three observations from the study itself point at what is actually missing
— and each one is answered by a standard that already exists. The relevant
body of work is **SQL-on-FHIR v2**, an HL7-community specification —
maintained under the FHIR project, with an official conformance test
suite — that makes FHIR resources queryable as tables. Its artifacts, plus
one plain-FHIR resource for vocabulary, are worth knowing by name because
they turn "extracting data from FHIR payloads" from a per-institution
engineering project into a set of small, versioned, shareable files.
FHIR4DS is a DuckDB-based engine that runs all of them — plus CQL and
FHIRPath — directly over FHIR payloads, in a notebook or in the browser.

**1. The FHIR analyses were only possible after a third party added a
terminology-mapping layer.** Raw FHIR payloads were not directly analyzable
by the research team without it. That is a correct diagnosis of the
*tooling*, not the data — and the vocabulary step is a solved problem in
the specification itself. A **ConceptMap** is a standard FHIR resource
whose entire job is "this code in this system means that concept" —
vocabulary translation as *data* rather than as a bespoke mapping program:

```json
{
  "resourceType": "ConceptMap",
  "group": [{
    "source": "http://hl7.org/fhir/administrative-gender",
    "target": "urn:omop:concept",
    "element": [
      { "code": "male",   "target": [{ "code": "8507", "equivalence": "equivalent" }] },
      { "code": "female", "target": [{ "code": "8532", "equivalence": "equivalent" }] }
    ]
  }]
}
```

Flatten it once with a ViewDefinition (next paragraph) and it is an
ordinary lookup table — `source_system + code → concept_id` — joined in a
query. No third party required: the mapping ships with the pipeline, and
the [OMOP CDM mapping library](/docs/user-guide/modeling/omop-mapping)
carries one covering 108,683 codes.

**2. The stated obstacle was "extracting data from FHIR payloads"** — a
data-engineering problem, not a clinical-content problem. And the gap stops
being expensive when the extraction becomes a **shareable standard
artifact**. The **ViewDefinition** is SQL-on-FHIR v2's reshaping artifact:
a small JSON file that declares, per resource type, which elements become
which columns, with FHIRPath expressions for paths — declarative and
precise rather than procedural code. Where an OMOP ETL is months of local
engineering, the same reshape is one of these, runnable by any conformant
engine against any FHIR export:

```json
{
  "name": "omop_person_source",
  "resource": "Patient",
  "select": [
    { "column": [
      { "name": "person_source_value", "path": "id", "type": "id" },
      { "name": "year_of_birth", "path": "birthDate.substring(0, 4)", "type": "string" },
      { "name": "gender_source_value", "path": "gender", "type": "code" }
    ] }
  ]
}
```

A **SQLQuery** then composes the views: a standard FHIR `Library` resource
whose body is ordinary SQL over the view names — joins, unions, and
derived columns live there, portable across engines. Write the mapping
once, version it, publish it, cite it — a mapping you *review and share*,
not one you rebuild at every institution. The
[FHIR → OMOP walkthrough](/docs/examples/fhir-to-omop) builds a complete
`person` table this way, live in the browser, and every other OMOP table is
the same recipe.

**3. FHIR captured what OMOP could not.** 6,300 participants had
multi-provider FHIR data versus 20 in OMOP, and 18.4% of timeline segments
existed *only* in FHIR — care delivered at outside institutions the
institutional ETL never saw. The study recommends FHIR as a *supplement*;
we agree, and would add that a supplement you cannot query is a supplement
you cannot use. Native FHIR querying — ViewDefinitions, CQL, FHIRPath,
straight over the payloads — makes the multi-provider slice analyzable the
day it lands, with no second ETL project. The two models compose rather
than compete: query FHIR natively for what only FHIR has, and project into
OMOP where the research ecosystem demands it, from the same standard
artifacts.

## Standards conformance, not vendor claims

Skeptics are right to ask what "spec compliant" means. Each of the
specifications FHIR4DS implements ships an HL7-community-built reference
implementation and an official test suite designed to validate other
implementations. FHIR4DS passes all of them — **2,785 official conformance
tests at 100%** in release 0.0.21:

| Suite | Tests | Passing |
|---|---|---|
| CQL — official HL7 `cql-tests` corpus | 1,706 | 100% |
| FHIRPath — official reference suite | 935 | 100% |
| SQL-on-FHIR v2 — official `spec_tests` | 144 | 100% |
| CMS eCQM measures (QI-Core 2025) — evaluated end-to-end | 47 | 100% |

The OMOP CDM v5.5 mapping library carries its own example suite (38
expected-output tests — OMOP has no official test suite), kept green in
the same runner.

The conformance runner ships in the repository — anyone can reproduce the
numbers.

## The bottom line

The All of Us study is a valuable census of what today's *default*
acquisition paths deliver, and its recommendation of FHIR as a supplement
to OMOP is sound for programs that already have both. But its own evidence
points past its framing: the cost it identified was not FHIR's ability to
represent clinical reality — it was the absence of native analysis tooling
for FHIR payloads. That absence is what FHIR4DS removes. As bulk exports
fatten under the USCDI v3 mandate (in force since January 2026) and
standard mapping artifacts accumulate, the "OMOP richer, FHIR raw"
tradeoff narrows from both ends — and the multi-provider data only FHIR
captures becomes queryable the day it arrives.
